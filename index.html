England & Wales Student Finance System: Essential Rules

Student loans in England and Wales behave unlike any traditional bank loan or mortgage. Understanding these rules is crucial before making any repayment or parental contribution decisions.

1. It is an Income-Contingent "Graduate Tax"

Under UK law, what you repay each month is 100% determined by what you earn, never by how much you owe. If you earn under the threshold (£25k on Plan 5, £27,295 on Plan 2), you pay exactly £0, regardless of whether your loan balance is £10,000 or £100,000.

2. The 30-Year & 40-Year Cancellation Rule

Any remaining loan balance is completely wiped clean by the UK government after 30 years (Plan 2) or 40 years (Plan 5) from the April following graduation. It does not affect credit ratings, and you never pass this debt to your heirs.

3. Plan 2 vs Plan 5 Comparison

  • Plan 2 (England 2012-23 / Wales 2012+): Threshold £27,295. Repayment 9% above threshold. Written off after 30 years. Interest is RPI to RPI+3%.
  • Plan 5 (England Aug 2023+): Threshold £25,000. Repayment 9% above threshold. Written off after 40 years. Interest is strictly flat RPI (0% real interest).

4. Why Paying Tuition Upfront Can Be a Mistake

If your child earns a typical graduate salary, they may never clear the full balance before the write-off date. Putting £27,750 into university fees means you just saved HMRC money! The student pays the exact same monthly deduction. Putting that £27,750 in a Lifetime ISA or Stocks ISA creates a huge house deposit instead.

Frequently Asked Questions for Parents & Students

Will having a student loan stop my child getting a mortgage?

No. Student debt is not recorded on credit files (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion). Mortgage lenders only assess student loans as a regular deduction from net monthly income (reducing monthly disposable income slightly), exactly like council tax or pension contributions.

What happens if my child loses their job or takes parental leave?

Student loan repayments immediately stop. If their salary drops below the threshold, deductions cease automatically without penalty, default notices, or debt collectors.

When does paying fees upfront actually make financial sense?

Paying fees upfront makes mathematical sense ONLY if the graduate is destined for top-decile earnings (e.g., corporate law, investment banking, software engineering at FAANG, or senior medicine), where they will clear the entire loan balance well before the 30- or 40-year write-off mark. In that specific scenario, reducing debt upfront prevents compounding RPI interest.
